# Investigation — The Sapotnitsky Name

*Opened 2026-10-10, after Phase 2 extraction. Question posed by Wendy: the name — the presumed natal surname of **Chaya, wife of Aryeh Leib "Leibe" Mostkoff (B02)**, Israel Mostkoff's mother — is mysterious and interesting; where does it come from and where does it fit?*

*Method: every appearance of the name and its variants in this corpus, then the origin hypotheses each body of evidence supports, then what would settle the question. Citations follow `data/people.md` conventions; confidence marks throughout. Companion records: EV-10, EV-36, EV-48, EV-13; conflicts CF-10, CF-17, and new **CF-29**.*

---

## 1. Why this name matters

Chaya is the earliest confirmed ancestress of the entire Mostkoff line, yet she has **no record of her own** — no birth, no marriage record we can prove, no burial entry, no property list `[Narrative, pp. 4–5, 9; B02]`. Her *existence* rests on the memoir `tania-project (§4)`; her *surname* rests on a **single document**: her son Israel's Mexican death certificate (EV-13, 1957). Everything else about "Sapotnitsky" in the corpus is hypothesis stacked on that one attestation. If the surname can be anchored to a real Belarusian family, it opens Chaya's natal line — and, through the Bunin-cousin thread (CF-10), possibly the family's only link to relatives who died in the Slutsk destruction of 1941.

## 2. The one primary record — and what it can and cannot prove

**"Israel Mostkoff Sapotnisky"** — so recorded on Israel's death certificate, Mexico City, 1 Oct 1957 (EV-13; cert image at Narrative, p. 23; Wendy's transcription of the name at Narrative, pp. 4–5; spelling in the file varies Saptonitsky/Sapotnisky/Sapotniskiy).

- **What it proves**: that Israel's children (the presumptive informants — Abram, Luis, Miguel, or Dora) supplied "Sapotnitsky" as the maternal surname in 1957, per the Mexican two-apellido convention (father's family + mother's family) — the convention Wendy herself invokes `Narrative, p. 5` `[C]`.
- **What it doesn't prove**: that the name was *accurately* transmitted. The chain is: Chaya (d. Slutsk, date unknown) → Israel (left Belarus 1924, d. 1957) → children (none of whom ever met their grandmother, as far as any source shows). It is one family's phonetic memory of a surname none of the informants ever used in life. That is real evidence — families do transmit grandmothers' maiden names — but it is **[L], not [C]**, and it carries a single point of failure.
- **The corroborating silence is significant**: the name appears in **no other family record** — not in Morduckh's 1906 Lida marriage or the 1907–13 Lida births of his children (EV-01, 19–21); not in Isodoro's naturalization-era material (Narrative, p. 11); not in the 1911 Vsia Rossiia entry, which does exactly what Mexican convention does — matronymic-by-father: "**Shifra Nakhman**[ovna] Mastkov" (EV-04) — proving the *practice* of carrying a mother's-family name existed in this family's records and that for the *next* generation it was Boruchovich, not Sapotnitsky, that surfaced; not on Israel's headstone ("son of Aryeh Leib" only — EV-15); and not once in Tania's memoir, which otherwise preserves the family's names in loving detail. One document, one country, thirty years and an ocean after anyone saw Chaya `[?]`.

## 4. The geography, resolved

The older translation stranded Wendy with "**Neswalok**", an unfindable village "near the Polish border" — and 197 miles away, Sapotskin, which *is* near a border but didn't fit anything else (Narrative, pp. 6–8). This is now settled by better sources:

- The memoir's **Russian original** says **Несвижа** — Nesvizh — outright (`russian.md`, the grandmother passage; the site's own gazetteer entry: "Несвиж (Nesvizh) | Nyasvizh, Minsk Region, Belarus | 53.22°N 26.69°E | Birthplace/home village of paternal grandmother Khaya, near the Polish border of the time"). "Neswalok" was a misreading of Несвиж `[C]`.
- The remaining tension — Nesvizh is *not* near today's Polish border — dissolves historically: the gazetteer's phrase "near the Polish border **of the time**" refers to the interwar (Riga, 1921) border, which ran just west of the Nesvizh/Kletsk line, putting the town close against Poland during Tania's childhood `[L — investigator's inference from general border history; verify against a 1921–39 map before relying on it]`.
- Consequence: **Sapotskin weakens as Chaya's home** (nothing places her family 197 miles west), but remains live as the *ultimate toponymic origin of the surname* — a family could carry a Sapotskin-coined name for a century while living near Nesvizh; the 1804 surname edict predates everyone in this story (Avotaynu quote, Narrative, pp. 8–9).

And the geography now *supports* the record cluster: the village is near Nesvizh, and it is Nesvizh — alone among all the towns in this corpus — that yields both a **Zaturensky family headed by a Zelik** (1851, EV-36; plus six men in the property lists, Narrative, p. 37) and an 1889 marriage bride whose **father Zelik came from Nesvizh** (EV-10). Chaya's village, the Zelik of the marriage record, and the 1851 Zaturensky-Zelik all point at the same small town `[L]`.

## 5. The 1889 marriage record — the two-Khayas reading

Wendy's identification (Notes, p. 12) founders on two hard facts she half-noted:

1. **The date**: the marriage is 28 Jul 1889; Israel, the couple's supposed son, was born in 1880 (memoir, twice — `tania-project (§§4, 35)`). Wendy flagged this ("could a religious marriage have been earlier?", EV-10) — possible, but unanchored.
2. **The age** (new observation, now logged as **CF-29**): the bride is **20** in 1889 → born ~1869. Our Chaya was mothering Israel by 1880 and is estimated b. ~1855–60 (B02) — she would have been ~27–34, not 20. **This is not Chaya.**

But the record need not be discarded. Read instead as the marriage of a **younger namesake cousin** — another Khaia of the same natal family (the cross-branch recycling of names is this family's signature problem; cf. two Taybas, two Gindas, two Dorises, and a niece Dveira-Dora who died the year her namesake was born) — everything reconciles: the family name, the Zelik father, the Nesvizh origin, and our Chaya as an *elder* daughter of the same clan, married earlier (~1875–76 per B02) to Leibe `[?] — plausible, unproven`. It even suggests a civil-marriage pattern (families regularizing religious marriages years later) that would explain the 1889 date without inventing a second marriage for Leibe.

Under either reading, the record to pull is the same: **NHAB Minsk/1520/7/4**, which Wendy's genealogy-society contact was already chasing (Notes, p. 12). The original would settle whether the bride's patronymic column reads "Berezino" (one garbled parse), "Zelik" (Wendy's), or something else — and, more importantly, what the surname actually renders.

My read of the balance: **H1 and H2 together, as one picture** — a natal family near Nesvizh whose name appears in Russian records as Zaturensky(?) and survived to 1957 Mexico as Sapotnitsky — is the most constructive working hypothesis, *provided* the phonetic gap can be bridged by the actual archive records; if it can't, H2 (an unremarkable local Sapotnitsky family) is the fallback. H3 is a footnote on surname etymology rather than family history. H4 stays open because of the Bunin hook. **CF-29** tracks the marriage-record question; the whole file remains `[?]` until a Belarusian record names Chaya's father.

- **CF-17 (Chaya's death/pinkas #870)**: unchanged, but note #870's Chaya-Henya is "wife of Zev Volf **Itskovits**" — *another* unexplained maternal-surname-shaped name floating around Chaya's identity; three competing "surnames" (Sapotnitsky, Zaturinskii, Itskovits) for one woman is itself evidence of how soft this corner of the tree is.
- **The Mostkoff side**: EV-10 also claims *Leibe's* identity (groom Leizer Min'kovich, father Zisman). The same two-Khayas skepticism applies — CF-29 covers both. Note the asymmetry: Israel's patronymic is rock-solid (**Leibovich**, headstone + memoir), so if the 1889 groom were Leibe, his father was Zisman — testable against nothing we currently hold.
